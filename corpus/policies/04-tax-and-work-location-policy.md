# Tax and Work Location Policy

## TAX-1 Why Work Location Matters

An employee's physical work location determines which state (and, if outside the United States, which country) has the right to tax their wages, and can create obligations for Northbridge to register as an employer in that jurisdiction. This is true even for short-term or temporary work locations, which is why work location changes are governed separately from, and in addition to, the Remote Work Policy (REMOTE-WORK). The Remote Work Policy describes the business process (who approves what, and when); this policy explains the underlying tax and legal reasons that process exists, so that employees understand why a request that seems purely logistical (for example, "I'd like to work from my parents' house for two months") can require review beyond a simple manager approval.

Work location is evaluated based on where an employee is physically performing work, not based on their home office assignment, their mailing address on file, or where their manager or team is located. An employee whose home office is Chicago but who is physically working from Colorado for an extended period is, for the purposes of this policy, working from Colorado.

## TAX-2 State Payroll Tax Registration

Northbridge is registered to run payroll and withhold state income tax in the following states: Illinois, Indiana, Wisconsin, Texas, California, New York, Washington, Colorado, Georgia, Florida, North Carolina, and Arizona (the "Registered States"). This list includes the state of every Northbridge office (Illinois, Texas, Colorado, and North Carolina) plus several additional states with a large existing remote workforce. An employee may work from any Registered State without triggering additional tax registration steps, subject to the approval and security requirements in REMOTE-WORK.

The list of Registered States is reviewed periodically by Finance based on where Northbridge's remote and hybrid employees are concentrated; a state is generally added to the list once the number of employees working from it, or credible pending demand to work from it, makes registration cost-effective relative to the compliance burden. Employees interested in a permanent move to a state not on this list should raise the request as early as possible, since adding a new Registered State is a larger undertaking than the temporary Remote Work Location Change process in RW-3 and is evaluated on a different timeline by Finance and Legal, generally as part of quarterly planning rather than in response to an individual request alone.

## TAX-3 Nexus and Business Registration Risk

Working from a state where Northbridge is not registered, even temporarily, can create "nexus" — a legal connection that may obligate the Company to register as a foreign business entity, register for payroll withholding, and potentially become subject to that state's corporate income tax. Registering in a new state typically takes 2 to 6 weeks and involves Finance and outside counsel, and in some cases involves an ongoing annual filing obligation that continues even after the specific employee who prompted the registration is no longer working from that state. For this reason, any request to work from a non-Registered State for more than 10 consecutive business days must be submitted at least 3 weeks in advance through the Remote Work Location Change request described in RW-3, so Finance and Legal can assess whether registration is feasible in the requested timeframe, and, if registration is not feasible within that timeframe, whether a modified (shorter, or delayed) arrangement can be approved instead.

Not every state creates the same level of registration burden; some states have simplified processes for employers with a small number of employees physically present, while others require a more involved process regardless of headcount. Finance evaluates each request based on the specific destination state's requirements rather than applying a uniform assumption, which is part of why individual review is required rather than a simple published list of "always denied" states.

## TAX-4 Employee Reporting Obligations

Employees must update their work location in the HR system within 5 business days of any change expected to last 10 or more consecutive business days, regardless of whether the new location is a Registered State. This reporting obligation exists independently of the approval process in RW-3: even a move to an already-Registered State should be reflected promptly in the HR system so that payroll withholding is calculated correctly and so that Finance has an accurate picture of where the Company's workforce is physically located for other compliance purposes (such as state-specific employment law notices).

Failure to report a location change can result in incorrect state tax withholding, which is the employee's responsibility to correct with the relevant state tax authority, and can expose the Company to unregistered nexus risk without its knowledge, which is a more serious concern from the Company's perspective because it removes Finance and Legal's ability to assess and manage the risk before it accumulates. An employee who is uncertain whether a particular change (for example, a lengthy visit to a family member's home) meets the 10-consecutive-business-day threshold should err on the side of asking People Operations or Finance rather than guessing.

## TAX-5 International Work Location and Tax Treaties

Working outside the United States creates substantially higher complexity than an interstate move: potential foreign payroll tax withholding, permanent establishment risk for the Company (the risk that a foreign government could treat the Company as having a taxable business presence in that country because of the employee's activity there), immigration and work-authorization requirements (many countries do not permit remote work on a tourist visa, regardless of who the work is for), and data residency considerations under SEC-3, which can be affected by local data protection law in the destination country.

Because of this complexity, Northbridge limits approved international remote work to a maximum of 20 business days per calendar year per employee, and only through the International Remote Work Exception process described in RW-4. This 20-business-day limit is deliberately conservative relative to some tax treaties' longer thresholds (many U.S. tax treaties use a 183-day rule for individual tax residency purposes), because the Company-level permanent establishment risk and immigration risk can arise well before an individual employee's personal tax residency would be affected, and because a single Company-wide limit is far simpler to apply consistently than a country-by-country analysis for every request.

Requests exceeding 20 business days in a single stretch or in aggregate for the year are not approved under the standard exception process and require a separate individual agreement negotiated by Legal, which is granted only in unusual circumstances, generally where there is an independent Company business reason for the extended international presence (such as a client engagement that specifically requires it) rather than solely a personal preference to work from abroad for longer than the standard limit allows.

## TAX-6 Consequences of Unreported Location Changes

An employee who works from an unreported or unapproved location may have their remote work arrangement suspended pending review, may be required to return to a Registered State or a Company office, and may be personally responsible for any resulting state tax liabilities or penalties, including the cost of correcting withholding after the fact with a state tax authority. Deliberate concealment of a work location change may be addressed under the Workplace Conduct policy (CONDUCT-1), since accurate reporting of work location is treated as part of the general expectation of honest communication with the Company described in CONDUCT-1, not merely an administrative formality.

## TAX-7 Illustrative Examples

The following examples are illustrative only and do not modify the rules above:

- An employee whose home office is Chicago (Illinois, a Registered State) takes a two-week trip to visit family in Wisconsin (also a Registered State) and works from there. Because Wisconsin is a Registered State, this falls under the ordinary allowance in RW-3 for stays of 10 business days or less if the trip is 10 business days or fewer; if the trip is longer than 10 consecutive business days, a Remote Work Location Change request is still required even though Wisconsin is already a Registered State, because the request-and-approval requirement in RW-3 for stays beyond 10 business days applies to any non-home location, not only non-Registered States. In practice, such a request is typically approved quickly since no new state registration is needed.
- An employee wants to work for three weeks from Oregon, which is not a Registered State. This requires a Remote Work Location Change request at least 3 weeks in advance so Finance can evaluate whether registering in Oregon is feasible in that timeframe; depending on Oregon's specific requirements and Finance's current workload, this may be approved, approved with adjusted dates, or denied with a recommendation to use a Registered State or shorten the stay to 10 business days or fewer in a state where that would not require a new registration (which Oregon, as a non-Registered State, still would).
- An employee wants to work remotely from Portugal for six weeks (approximately 30 business days) to combine a family visit with continued work. Because this is international and exceeds the 20-business-day cap in TAX-5, it is not approvable under the standard International Remote Work Exception process regardless of how far in advance it is requested; the employee's options are to shorten the international portion to 20 business days or fewer and use PTO for the remainder, or to discuss a possible individual agreement with Legal if there is an independent Company business reason for the longer stay.
